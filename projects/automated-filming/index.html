Engineering decisions

The algorithm has to move a physical assembly

The team combined a Raspberry Pi, camera, two servos, infrared receiver, and a ball-transfer support. The support distributes the load and reduces friction. The software then has to translate an image-space target into motion the pan–tilt mechanism can execute.

Pixels become pulse widths through an explicit controller

The largest face supplies a center point. Its normalized offset drives incremental pan and tilt commands, with angle limits and a calibrated pulse-width mapping. Although the source contains derivative terms, their gains are zero in the final code. The implemented visual controller therefore uses proportional feedback.

A usable prototype includes the operator

A separate thread handles infrared commands while the vision loop runs. The operator can switch modes, move either axis, and stop the device. The prototype won the HD Hyundai Mechatronics Grand Prize. The demos show its behavior; quantitative tracking-error and settling-time results are not supplied.

Implementation notes & source evidence

The mechanism and the control objective

A solo creator needs the subject to stay framed while the camera moves smoothly enough to remain usable. The team combined a Raspberry Pi, a wide-view camera, two pan–tilt servos, an infrared receiver, and a ball-transfer support. The support reduces friction and distributes the assembly’s load; the two axes provide horizontal and vertical reframing.

From pixels to actuator commands

The final program uses Picamera2 and OpenCV’s Haar-cascade detector, selecting the largest detected face. Its horizontal and vertical offsets from the image center are normalized by frame dimensions and converted into incremental servo-angle commands. pigpio maps those commands to servo pulse widths with an explicit offset, pulse cap, and angle limits.

Sources: Vision-to-servo control loop

What the controller actually implements

The source contains proportional and derivative terms, but both derivative gains are set to zero in final.py. The checked-in implementation therefore acts as proportional image-error feedback, rather than demonstrating a tuned PID controller. The stored angle is the commanded angle; there is no separate measured joint-angle feedback in this program. This distinction separates visual feedback from the servo’s internal position regulation.

Sources: Gains and servo command mapping

Manual control and device state

A separate thread reads LIRC’s irw output so remote commands can switch automatic/manual operation, move either axis, stop, or exit while the vision loop continues. An LED indicates face detection. On shutdown, the program terminates the remote process, stops the camera, and disables servo pulses. This joins the tracking algorithm to an actual device interaction loop.

Sources: Remote input and cleanup

Result and engineering takeaway

The team received the 2024 HD Hyundai Mechatronics Competition Grand Prize and a KRW 3,000,000 team prize. The project’s engineering value lies in connecting camera coordinates, control gains, calibrated pulse widths, mechanical support, and user input. The linked demos show prototype behavior; the repository does not supply quantitative tracking-error or settling-time measurements.
